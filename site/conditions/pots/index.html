Is POTS partly autoimmune?

Immune findings have created important hypotheses, but researchers are still working out whether they cause illness, follow it, or define only some forms of POTS.

CLINICAL TRIALS6 min readReviewed September 17, 2026
Key takeaways

Some studies report immune abnormalities in people with POTS.

No antibody test currently establishes POTS or proves an autoimmune cause.

What is it?

Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome, or POTS, is defined by an excessive sustained heart-rate increase while upright, alongside chronic orthostatic symptoms and after other explanations are considered.

Why does it matter?

Symptoms can be disabling, and treatment is often based on managing circulation, heart rate, and specific contributors. A confirmed immune mechanism could eventually support more targeted therapies.

What did researchers find?

Small studies have reported autoantibodies and other immune differences. These findings are not consistent enough to show that autoimmunity causes POTS across patients.

Where is the science right now?

Clinical trials are evaluating immune-modifying approaches in selected participants. A trial tests whether a treatment works under defined conditions; it does not establish that the approach is effective.

Is this available to patients?

Standard symptom-management approaches are available. Immune therapies for POTS remain investigational outside other established indications.

What are the limitations?

POTS is heterogeneous. Studies are often small, biomarkers are unsettled, and positive antibodies can also appear in people without POTS.

What happens next?

Larger controlled studies need to identify reproducible biomarkers and determine whether any immune-defined subgroup benefits from targeted treatment.

Sources

  • NIH research context on infection-associated chronic illness
  • New England Journal of Medicine: POTS review
  • ClinicalTrials.gov: active and completed POTS studies

What This Means

The immune hypothesis is scientifically plausible and actively studied. It is not yet a settled explanation or a basis for self-treatment.

What We Still Don’t Know

Researchers do not know whether immune findings define a distinct subgroup, how to identify it reliably, or whether changing those findings improves symptoms.
